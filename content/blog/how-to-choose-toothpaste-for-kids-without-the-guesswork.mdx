Working out how to choose toothpaste for kids comes down to four things: the fluoride level matches their age, the amount used matches the ppm, the flavour is one they'll actually tolerate twice a day, and the tube size fits how your household goes through it. Everything else on the packaging, the colours, the cartoon characters, the whitening claims, is secondary. Get those four right and brushing stops being a fight.

Most parents end up choosing toothpaste by process of elimination: whatever's left on the shelf, whatever the child didn't spit out last time, whatever's cheapest that week. That's understandable in a shared family bathroom where four people are getting through tubes at different rates. But a few minutes spent checking labels properly saves you buying the wrong strength, or buying six tubes of a flavour nobody will touch.

## How to choose toothpaste for kids: the features that actually matter

Strip away the marketing and a kids' toothpaste only needs to do a few jobs well:

- **Contain the right fluoride level for the child's age.** This is the one non-negotiable.
- **Be gentle enough for daily use**, which is usually described as 'enamel-safe' on the label.
- **Taste good enough that brushing doesn't become a negotiation.**
- **Come in a size and pack count that matches how fast your household gets through a tube.**

Vegan and cruelty-free are increasingly common claims too, and worth checking if that matters to your family, but they don't affect how well the toothpaste cleans or protects teeth. For a fuller breakdown of what 'vegan toothpaste' actually means on a label, see [how to check if toothpaste is vegan before you buy](/blog/how-to-check-if-toothpaste-is-vegan-before-you-buy).

## Fluoride levels: the label check that matters most

Fluoride is what actually protects teeth, so this is where to focus your attention rather than the flavour or the packaging. The NHS guidance is specific and worth following rather than guessing at:

- **Under 3 years old:** a smear of toothpaste, using a fluoride level of no less than 1,000ppm.
- **Age 3 to 6:** a pea-sized amount, using a toothpaste with more than 1,000ppm fluoride.
- **Adults and older children:** toothpaste with 1,350 to 1,500ppm fluoride.

Brushing should happen twice a day for about two minutes, last thing before bed and at one other time, and the advice is to spit out after brushing rather than rinse with water, so the fluoride stays on the teeth a bit longer.

Always check the ppm figure printed on the tube or pack before buying, since this isn't something to assume from the brand or the fact that it's marketed for kids. If you'd like a plain explanation of what the numbers mean and where to find them, [how to read fluoride levels on toothpaste](/blog/how-to-read-fluoride-levels-on-toothpaste) and [what does fluoride do for teeth](/blog/fluoride-toothpaste-explained) both walk through it in more detail. If you're ever unsure which strength suits your child's age, a dentist, health visitor or pharmacist can advise.

## Flavour, whitening claims and the things worth weighing up

Once the fluoride level is sorted, flavour is genuinely the next biggest factor in whether brushing happens without a fuss. A child who hates the taste of mint toothpaste will find ways to avoid brushing, rush it, or refuse the second session of the day. Fruity and sweet flavours such as watermelon, grape, peach, berry, gummy bear and candy flavours give you somewhere else to go if mint has become a battleground. For more on matching flavour to a fussy brusher, see [how to pick a toothpaste flavour for kids](/blog/how-to-pick-a-toothpaste-flavour-for-kids) and [mint-free toothpaste for kids](/blog/toothpaste-for-kids-who-hate-mint).

Whitening claims are worth understanding rather than dismissing or over-trusting. Whitening toothpaste works by removing surface stains through mild abrasion or polishing, it doesn't change the natural colour of the tooth underneath. That's a reasonable, useful job for everyday brushing, just don't expect a dramatic shade change. [Does whitening toothpaste work](/blog/does-whitening-toothpaste-work) covers this properly, and [is whitening toothpaste safe for kids](/blog/is-whitening-toothpaste-safe-for-kids) is worth a read if you're deciding whether the same tube suits the whole family.

'Enamel-safe' is another phrase to look for rather than skip past. It generally signals the formula is gentle enough for daily use without being too abrasive on developing or sensitive teeth. [Enamel safe toothpaste explained](/blog/enamel-safe-toothpaste-explained) sets out what to check for if you want to understand the term properly before buying.

## Frequently asked questions

### What fluoride level should children's toothpaste have?

The NHS recommends a smear of toothpaste with no less than 1,000ppm fluoride for children under 3, a pea-sized amount with more than 1,000ppm for ages 3 to 6, and 1,350 to 1,500ppm for adults and older children. Always check the figure printed on the tube, since it isn't the same across every brand.

### Is flavoured toothpaste as effective as mint toothpaste?

Flavour doesn't affect how well a fluoride toothpaste protects teeth. What matters is the fluoride level and whether the child actually brushes properly and regularly, so a flavour they enjoy can be the more effective choice in practice if it gets them brushing twice a day without fuss.

### Does whitening toothpaste actually whiten teeth?

It removes surface stains through gentle polishing, which can make teeth look brighter, but it doesn't change the natural colour of the tooth underneath. It's a reasonable everyday choice rather than a dramatic whitening treatment.

### How much toothpaste should my child use?

A smear for children under 3, and a pea-sized amount from age 3 to 6, matched to a toothpaste with the correct fluoride level for that age. [How much toothpaste should a child use](/blog/how-much-toothpaste-should-kids-use) covers this age by age if you want more detail.

### Are bigger toothpaste multipacks worth buying?

Generally yes, if your household gets through tubes regularly. Bigger bundles tend to cost less per tube than buying singles, and keeping a spare on hand avoids running out mid-week. Compare the price per tube on Amazon before deciding how many you need.
