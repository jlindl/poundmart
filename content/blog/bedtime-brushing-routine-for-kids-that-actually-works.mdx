A good bedtime brushing routine for kids is short, predictable and happens at the same point in the evening every single night: straight after the last snack or drink, before pyjamas or straight after, with the same two steps every time (brush, spit, no rinse) and a toothpaste flavour your child will actually tolerate. The routine matters more than any gadget or gimmick. Once it's a fixed part of the evening rather than a negotiation, most of the nightly battle disappears on its own.

This isn't about getting it perfect. It's about getting it to happen, calmly, most nights, for years. That's what protects teeth. Here's a version of the routine that holds up against tiredness, tantrums, school mornings and more than one child needing a turn.

## Bedtime brushing routine for kids: what actually works

The NHS guidance is the backbone of any routine: brush twice a day for about two minutes, once last thing at night and once at one other time in the day, then spit out the toothpaste rather than rinsing with water. Rinsing washes away the fluoride that's doing the work, so spit and leave it.

The amount of toothpaste depends on age:

- Under 3: a smear, using a toothpaste with no less than 1,000ppm fluoride.
- Age 3 to 6: a pea-sized amount, using a toothpaste with more than 1,000ppm fluoride.
- Adults and older children: a toothpaste with 1,350 to 1,500ppm fluoride.

Beyond the fluoride itself, three things make the evening routine stick:

1. **Same time, same order, every night.** The routine becomes automatic once it's not up for discussion.
2. **Someone supervises or helps** until a child can be trusted to brush properly alone, usually well into primary school.
3. **The toothpaste doesn't get in the way.** If a child spits it out, gags on the flavour or refuses the tube outright, the best brushing technique in the world won't get used.

## Making the routine survive school mornings and holidays

The bedtime brush is only half the NHS recommendation. The other brush needs to happen at some point in the day, and for most families that's the morning, in the middle of getting everyone out of the door.

A few things make the morning brush more likely to actually happen rather than get skipped:

- Keep the toothbrush and toothpaste somewhere a child can reach without help, so brushing isn't bottlenecked on an adult being free.
- Pair it with something that's already fixed in the morning, straight after breakfast works for most households, so it's attached to an existing habit rather than floating free.
- Replace toothbrushes roughly every three months, sooner if the bristles are splayed. A worn brush does a worse job and gives kids another reason to rush through it.

Holidays and school trips are where routines usually slip, mostly because the toothpaste gets left behind or runs out halfway through the week. Packing more than you think you need, rather than exactly enough, avoids a last-minute pharmacy run in an airport or a caravan park shop. If you're flying, airline liquid rules vary by airport and do change, so check your departure airport's current rules before you pack toothpaste in hand luggage.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a bedtime brushing routine actually take?

About two minutes of brushing, as recommended by the NHS, plus whatever time it takes to get a reluctant child to the sink. The brushing itself doesn't need to be longer than two minutes to be effective; consistency night after night matters more than stretching the time out.

### What if my child swallows a bit of toothpaste?

A small amount swallowed occasionally during normal brushing isn't usually a concern, which is part of why the NHS sets age-based amounts (a smear under 3, a pea-sized amount from 3 to 6). If you're worried about a specific amount or a child eating toothpaste outside of brushing, speak to a pharmacist or your GP.

### Does whitening toothpaste actually whiten children's teeth?

Whitening toothpaste works on surface stains, lifting marks from food and drink rather than changing the natural colour of the teeth underneath. It's a reasonable everyday toothpaste for the whole family, but it isn't a substitute for a dentist if a child has staining or colour changes that concern you.

### My child still won't brush at all. What now?

Try changing one variable at a time rather than everything at once, flavour first, since that's often the biggest sticking point, then timing, then who's doing the supervising. If a child consistently refuses or you're worried about pain or sensitivity behind the refusal, a dentist visit is the right next step rather than persisting with a routine that clearly isn't working.

### Is one toothpaste tube enough for the whole family?

It can be, since Nice Smile is described as suitable for both kids and adults, but check the fluoride ppm on the pack against the NHS age bands before assuming one tube suits everyone in the house. Where ages differ widely, it's often simpler to keep a couple of flavours on hand so each child has one they'll actually use.
