A good brushing routine comes down to three things: brushing twice a day for about two minutes, using the right amount of fluoride toothpaste for your age, and making it easy enough that nobody skips it when the house is chaotic. Everything else, flavours, electric versus manual, timers and stickers, is there to help you stick to those three things, not replace them.

Most households already know the theory. The problem is Tuesday night, when the youngest is overtired, the toothpaste tube is mysteriously empty, and someone has to get up for school in seven hours. A brushing routine only works if it survives those nights, not just the calm ones.

## A brushing routine that works for every age

The NHS guidance is the same whatever toothpaste you use: brush twice a day, last thing at night and at one other time, for about two minutes, then spit, don't rinse. Rinsing with water afterwards washes away the fluoride that's doing the work, so it's one of the easiest habits to fix if you're currently rinsing out of habit.

The amount of toothpaste and the fluoride strength change by age:

- Under 3s: a smear of toothpaste with no less than 1,000ppm fluoride.
- Ages 3 to 6: a pea-sized amount with more than 1,000ppm fluoride.
- Adults: toothpaste with 1,350 to 1,500ppm fluoride.

Check the pack for the ppm figure, since it varies between products and isn't always obvious from the front of the tube. If you're not sure what's right for a particular age or a particular child, a dentist or pharmacist can tell you in a minute and it's worth asking rather than guessing. For more on how much to use at each age, there's a fuller breakdown in [how much toothpaste should a child use](/blog/how-much-toothpaste-should-kids-use).

## Where routines actually fall apart

It's rarely the two-minute rule that trips people up. It's usually one of these:

- **Running out mid-week.** One tube per person means someone is always down to the last scrape by Wednesday, and a missed top-up at the shop turns into three missed brushes.
- **A shared bathroom bottleneck.** In a family bathroom used by four people before school, the routine competes with hair, breakfast and finding a second shoe. If brushing is the thing that gets rushed or skipped, it's worth looking at the order of the morning rather than the toothpaste.
- **Kids who just won't engage.** Flavour, bribery and timers all help, but a child who hates the taste of mint will fight you on it every single night.
- **Treating brushing as optional once teeth 'feel clean'.** Plaque builds up whether or not it feels obvious, which is why the twice-a-day habit matters more than how clean a mouth feels in the moment.
- **Forgetting the toothbrush itself.** A worn brush doesn't clean properly even with the right toothpaste and the right timing. The simple rule is to replace it every three months, or sooner if the bristles are splayed. There's more detail in [how often should you replace your toothbrush](/blog/when-to-replace-your-toothbrush).

If your household recognises more than one of these, it's worth fixing them one at a time rather than overhauling the whole routine at once.

## Keeping the routine going long term

A brushing routine that lasts isn't about getting one perfect week in. It's about making the easy path the correct one: toothpaste nobody minds the taste of, a fresh brush every three months, enough stock that running out isn't a weekly crisis, and the same two times a day, every day, even when it's rushed. Build those in and the two-minute habit mostly takes care of itself.

For younger children specifically, it also helps to make brushing feel like something they do rather than something done to them, within reason and with an adult supervising younger children's brushing. ## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a brushing routine take each time?

About two minutes, twice a day. It's longer than most people actually brush without a timer, so a two-minute song, an app or a simple kitchen timer can help make the habit stick, particularly with children.

### Should you rinse with water after brushing?

No. Spit out the excess toothpaste but don't rinse with water straight afterwards, since rinsing washes away the fluoride that's left on your teeth doing the work.

### Does whitening toothpaste change the colour of your teeth?

Whitening toothpaste removes surface staining from things like tea, coffee and food, which can make teeth look brighter, but it doesn't change their natural underlying colour. For more detail on what it can and can't do, see [does whitening toothpaste work](/blog/does-whitening-toothpaste-work).

### What if a child refuses to brush at all?

Try changing one thing at a time, flavour, letting them hold the brush themselves, or brushing alongside them so it's a shared activity rather than a solo chore. If refusal continues or you're worried about pain or decay, speak to a dentist, who can check what's going on and advise properly.
