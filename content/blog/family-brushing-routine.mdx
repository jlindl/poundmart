A good family brushing routine is simple: everyone brushes twice a day for about two minutes, last thing at night and at one other time, ideally together, with the right toothpaste for each age and supplies that never run out. The trick to making it stick is anchoring brushing to things that already happen every day, like getting dressed and putting on pyjamas.

Families don't struggle with brushing because they don't know how. They struggle because mornings are rushed, bedtimes are tired and someone always seems to have finished the toothpaste. This guide gives you a sample routine, a bathroom set-up that makes brushing the easy option, and a plan for keeping it going on the chaotic days.

## What every family brushing routine needs

Before you build the routine, get the foundations right. These come from NHS guidance and apply to everyone in the house:

- **Twice a day, about two minutes each time.** One of those should be last thing at night.
- **Fluoride toothpaste at the right strength and amount.** A smear with no less than 1,000ppm for under-3s, a pea-sized amount with more than 1,000ppm for 3 to 6 year olds, and 1,350 to 1,500ppm for adults.
- **Spit, don't rinse.** It leaves fluoride on the teeth for longer.
- **Supervision for younger children.** The NHS advises helping or watching children brush until they're at least 7.
- **A fresh toothbrush every three months**, or sooner if the bristles are worn. Our guide on [when to replace your toothbrush](/blog/when-to-replace-your-toothbrush) covers the signs.
- **Regular dental check-ups.** NHS dental care is free for children, and your dentist will tell each of you how often to come back.

Everything else, from the time you brush to which song you play, can flex around your family.

## A sample family brushing routine

The NHS recommends brushing last thing at night and at one other time, so choose the morning slot that suits your household and keep it the same every day. Here's one way it can look.

| Step | Morning | Bedtime |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anchor | After getting dressed, before shoes and coats | After bath and pyjamas, before stories |
| Toothpaste | An adult squeezes the right amount for each child | Same again, then the tube goes away |
| Brushing | About two minutes, with a song or timer | About two minutes, with a song or timer |
| Check | Adult finishes off for under-7s | Adult finishes off for under-7s |
| Afterwards | Spit, don't rinse | Spit, don't rinse, then nothing but water until morning |

### Why anchoring works

Brushing "at some point before school" relies on someone remembering. Brushing "straight after getting dressed" happens automatically because getting dressed always happens. The same goes for bedtime: tie brushing to pyjamas or the end of bath time, not to the moment everyone's already flagging.

### Fitting in older children and adults

Teenagers and grown-ups don't need supervising, but brushing at the same time as younger children is one of the easiest ways to show them it matters. If your mornings are staggered, keep bedtime as the shared brush.

## Brush together: why it helps

Children copy what they see. When a parent brushes alongside them, brushing stops being something done to them and becomes something the whole family does. It's also the easiest way to keep an eye on technique without hovering.

A few ways to make shared brushing work:

- **Youngest first.** Brush or finish off the littlest one's teeth, then brush your own while older children brush theirs.
- **Use one timer for everyone.** A song on a speaker or a sand timer in the middle of the sink keeps the whole family on the same two minutes.
- **Make it a bit silly.** Pulling faces in the mirror or a "who can spit into the plughole" contest keeps it light.

## Set up the bathroom so brushing is the easy option

The fewer obstacles between a child and their toothbrush, the more likely brushing is to happen.

- **One brush per person, colour coded.** Arguments about whose brush is whose vanish when everyone has their own colour.
- **Store brushes upright and apart.** Let them air dry, and keep the heads from touching each other.
- **Create a toothpaste station.** Keep toothpaste in one agreed spot, out of reach of younger children, so the grown-up in charge can find it in seconds.
- **Add a step or stool.** It helps small children reach the sink and see what they're doing in the mirror.
- **Keep spares in the cupboard.** A spare tube and a spare brush for everyone means no last-minute dashes to the shop.

## Keeping the routine going when life gets busy

Routines are easy on a quiet Tuesday. These are the moments that test them:

- **Holidays and weekends.** Keep brushing tied to the same anchors (getting dressed, pyjamas) even when the times shift. A wash bag with a spare tube and a brush for each person makes travel days easier.
- **Sleepovers and grandparents.** Pack each child's own brush and toothpaste so the routine travels with them.
- **Late nights.** On nights when everyone's shattered, do a calm, quick brush rather than skipping it. Bedtime brushing is the one to protect.
- **Illness.** If a child is poorly, keep brushing gently where you can, and ask your GP, pharmacist or dentist if anything seems painful.

## The bottom line

A family brushing routine that sticks isn't complicated. Anchor brushing to things that already happen every morning and night, brush together, set the bathroom up so everything's within reach, and keep a stash of toothpaste so you never run out. Get those in place and brushing stops being a daily reminder and starts being just what your family does.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should parents brush their teeth with their children?

Yes, if you can. Children learn by copying, so brushing together shows them brushing is normal and lets you model good technique. It also makes supervision easy, which the NHS advises until children are at least 7.

### What time should a family brush their teeth at night?

The NHS advises brushing last thing at night, so aim for after the last food or drink of the day. Straight after bath and pyjamas, before stories, is a good slot. After that, stick to water until the morning.

### Can the whole family use the same toothpaste?

Often, yes. A family toothpaste with 1,350 to 1,500ppm fluoride suits adults and also meets the NHS guidance for young children, provided they use the right amount for their age. Check the ppm on the pack, and ask your dentist if anyone has specific needs.

### How do I keep teenagers brushing twice a day?

Give them ownership: their own brush, their own toothpaste flavour and responsibility for replacing them. Gentle reminders help, and regular dental check-ups reinforce the message from someone other than you.
